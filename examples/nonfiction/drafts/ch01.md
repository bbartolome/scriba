# Chapter 1: The Moment It Clicked

Ask a violinist when she first felt she was getting better, and she will rarely name a concert. She will name a small afternoon in a rented room, a passage she had played a hundred times, and the sudden sense that her bow arm was doing something it had never done before.

A surgeon described something similar. He had trained for years, and the operations had become routine. Then a senior colleague watched him close a wound and said, quietly, that he was rushing the last two stitches. He had not noticed. Once he did, he could not stop noticing, and the next dozen operations were different.

A chess player told a third version. He had lost a game to a player rated far below him, and the loss made him replay every move. The mistake was not tactical. He had stopped looking at the board after the opening and was playing from habit.

These stories share a shape. There is an ordinary stretch of practice, a flash of attention to a specific error, and then a change that lasts. This book is about that shape. It asks what produced the flash, how to produce it on purpose, and why most practice never does.

The question matters because most people assume that getting better is a matter of time. Give it enough years, the assumption goes, and skill will follow. The violinist, the surgeon and the chess player all had years. What they lacked, for a long stretch, was a reason to look closely at what they were doing.
