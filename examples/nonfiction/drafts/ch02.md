# Chapter 2: Why Practice Stops Working

Repetition feels like progress. You do the task, it goes reasonably well, and you do it again. After a few months the results stop changing. You still practice, and you still get the same score.

This is the plateau. It is not a sign that the ceiling of your talent has been reached. It is usually a sign that the task has become easy enough to perform without thinking about it.

Consider a runner who trains on the same loop every morning at the same pace. Her body adapts quickly, and within a few weeks the loop costs her almost nothing. She is fitter than she was, but the workout no longer asks her to become fitter. A coach would change the distance, the hills or the pace, so that the effort was once again beyond her comfort.

The same logic applies to writing, to language learning and to any craft with a visible result. A writer who drafts the same kind of paragraph every day gets fluent at that paragraph. The writer who tries a harder structure, or who rewrites a paragraph three times to test a single idea, is doing something different.

The uncomfortable part is that the change has to be specific. Vague goals such as "get better at writing" give no direction for the next session. A better goal names the weakness: transitions between sections, or the first sentence of each scene, or the way a claim is supported. Then the session has a target and the attempt has a result you can check.
